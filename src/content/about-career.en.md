After several years of working with the government, e-commerce seemed like a much more human kind of business. Clear opportunities for growth, the prospect of building a steady stream of orders, and a lot more room for entrepreneurship.

I was 24, one of my financial partners owned a money-losing online store, and I pictured myself as the guy who would come in and turn it into a business.
Somehow

<!-- prose -->

The owner introduced me to the staff with the words: "This is Nikolai. He'll be helping us develop the store." Most of the staff were Siberian guys in their fifties. Still, I was the one expected to make the key commercial decisions and see that they were carried out.

About six months later, the war began. On the morning of February 24, I read the news and went to work. Everyone was silent. For the first few hours, we didn't even discuss what was happening.

During the first few weeks, we occasionally had to suspend sales across every channel. Sell a refrigerator for 30,000 rubles today, and tomorrow you might discover that it now costs 50,000 just to buy one from the supplier. There were opportunities to make good money in the chaos, but most of it came down to daily crisis management. Plans for growing the business had to be put aside for a while.

That first year was a good commercial education. The owner had two decades of experience in retail. In public procurement, I'd grown used to operating fairly aggressively, relying on my own ingenuity to get out of trouble. He taught me commercial discipline: to evaluate my own initiatives more thoroughly, pay closer attention to how I spent resources, and stop creating problems I could then heroically solve.

Home appliances are a low-margin business, and I wanted to grow revenue without growing the team along with it. I made up for my lack of commercial experience with analytics: studying sales by brand, product category, and region, looking for patterns, and using what I found to make decisions about assortment, purchasing, and promotion.

The employees handled what I couldn't automate, while much of the day-to-day operation gradually moved into software. We didn't have much of a formal management structure, but our planning meetings over a bottle of whiskey were remarkably productive.

I was never particularly happy with off-the-shelf e-commerce software. Once I discovered n8n and learned how to work with LLMs, I started building some of my own tools. At first, all I wanted was to get rid of routine work. But at some point, I decided I might be able to turn this into a separate business.

For my last six months at the store, I was also designing an analytics system built around the Ozon API. I wanted to create an AI assistant that understood how a retail business worked and could actually work with its data.

The funny thing is that much of what I'd set out to build turned out not to need AI at all. I built my own relational database, automated a number of analytical reports, and was still trying to figure out where it actually made sense to stick an LLM

My last ten months in e-commerce were hell. Suppliers were going bankrupt. Others were imposing pricing policies on us while competing against us on the same marketplaces. And customer behavior in a wartime economy seemed to defy conventional business logic.

By then, we'd built a profitable business generating around $1.5 million in annual revenue, and I still had plenty of room to grow as an entrepreneur. But continuing to run it was making less and less commercial sense.

Then the state took an interest not in my entrepreneurial abilities, but in me personally.

I received a military summons and decided to leave Russia.

A year later, Ukrainian forces destroyed Russia's largest marketplace, so I can't say I have many regrets about the end of my e-commerce career.

---

After leaving Russia, I had to figure out what I wanted to do with my life all over again. I had absolutely no desire to continue working in e-commerce. I had 1.5 million rubles, a pretty strong desire to make something of myself in IT, and no real plan whatsoever.

One of my first commercial ideas was a tool for visualizing modular furniture in a customer's own home. I was exploring ComfyUI at the time, looking for businesses that could make use of it. Modular sofas seemed promising: a limited number of structurally similar components could be combined into all sorts of configurations. Customers could design their own sofas and immediately see what they would look like in their homes. And with so few base components, I figured I could get consistent results. After two weeks of experiments, I realized I couldn't get the visualizations accurate or convincing enough. Not yet, anyway.

Around the same time, a colleague suggested I build a porn bot. Thanks to some artist friends, I knew a little about how the NSFW industry worked and understood that there was money to be made. Besides, I figured it wouldn't take very long to build.

After looking at the competition, we decided that the conversations in existing products probably weren't good enough to keep people coming back. We didn't want to build a business around selling people their first subscription. I needed to develop a dialogue system that could give characters their own behavior and make interacting with them interesting enough that users would want to return.

Once I'd developed the dialogue technology, I reached an agreement with the owners of a tattoo studio to build an automated lead qualification system. The two projects had completely different jobs to do. In one, I needed to imitate human conversation and entertain people. In the other, I needed to interpret what customers wanted in the context of specific business processes. It turned out I could use the same technical approach for two very different commercial purposes.

<!-- callout -->

When my dialogue engine started working, I realized I could design fairly complex software systems on my own. But the problem itself was so unlike conventional software development that I had trouble figuring out where my skills fit into any familiar engineering discipline. I wasn't even sure how much of what I was doing counted as regular software engineering.

<!-- prose -->

That changed when I built my own backend in TypeScript. This was a much more conventional engineering problem, one where I could actually judge whether I'd done a good job. By then, I'd designed a system combining a relational database, workflow orchestration, a dialogue engine, a generative media pipeline, and an application backend.
